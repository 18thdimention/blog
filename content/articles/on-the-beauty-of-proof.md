There is a particular kind of silence that follows a completed proof — not the silence of absence, but of arrival. You have been walking through fog, and suddenly the ground beneath you is solid. The landscape reveals itself all at once.

## Why proofs feel like poems

A good proof, like a good poem, says exactly what it needs to and nothing more. Every line carries weight. Remove one, and the structure collapses. Add one unnecessarily, and the elegance dissolves into noise.

Euclid understood this. His proof of the infinitude of primes is barely a paragraph, yet it has outlasted empires. There is no ornamentation, no hedging. Just the quiet certainty of logic unfolding.

## The role of intuition

Before the proof comes the conjecture — the moment of seeing something that might be true. This is where mathematics becomes art. The formal proof is the frame; the conjecture is the painting.

I have always been drawn to the conjectures that feel inevitable, the ones where the truth seems to shimmer just out of reach. The Riemann Hypothesis has this quality. So does the Collatz conjecture. They are statements so simple a child could understand them, yet they resist the full machinery of modern mathematics.

## On writing mathematics

Writing mathematics is an act of translation — from the private language of intuition to the shared language of symbols and logic. The difficulty is not in the destination but in choosing the right path.

Some proofs proceed by contradiction: assume the opposite, walk until you hit a wall, and conclude that you must have started in the wrong direction. Others are constructive: here is the object, here are its properties, watch as they unfold.

I prefer the constructive ones. There is something deeply satisfying about building rather than demolishing.

## A small observation

Consider the sequence 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21...

Each number is the sum of the two before it. Simple enough. But divide each number by its predecessor and you approach the golden ratio — an irrational number that appears in sunflower spirals, Greek architecture, and the proportions of the human face.

Mathematics is full of these moments: you follow a simple rule, and the universe opens up.

## Closing thought

I write because mathematics deserves to be witnessed, not just practiced. A beautiful proof locked in a journal is like a symphony no one plays. The notes are all there, but the music is missing.

So I write. Not to explain — though explanation has its place — but to share the particular joy of seeing something clearly for the first time.
